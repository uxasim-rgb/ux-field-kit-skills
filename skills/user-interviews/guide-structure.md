# Guide structure

A working template, the reasoning behind the order, and a question bank to adapt.

## The template

Times assume a 60-minute session. Scale proportionally; below 30 minutes, cut the context section rather than the core.

### Before (not in the session)

- Participant ID, segment, how they were recruited, screener answers.
- The one thing this session must produce. If the session runs long, this is what survives.
- Any hypothesis you hold, written down. Writing it down makes it easier to notice when you are fishing for it.

### Warm-up — 5 min

Purpose: establish that this is a conversation, that there are no wrong answers, and that they are the expert. Every question here must be impossible to fail.

- Consent script, recording confirmation. See `research-planning`.
- "Tell me about your role — what does a normal week look like?"
- "Where does [the domain thing] fit into that?"

Do not ask anything you need an answer to yet. The participant is still calibrating how much detail to give.

### Context — 10 min

Purpose: the surrounding system. Who else is involved, what happens upstream and downstream, what tools are already in play. Most of the best findings live here rather than in the core, because this is where the workarounds are.

- "Walk me through the last time you did [the task]. Start from what triggered it."
- "Who else was involved?"
- "What did you have to have ready before you could start?"
- "What happened after you finished — where did it go?"

### The core — 25 min

Purpose: the research questions. Two or three topics maximum. Each opens with a past-event question and then gets probed down.

Structure each topic as: opening question → listen → probe the most interesting thread → return for the second thread if time.

Leave the topics in a fixed order but let the participant reorder them by what they raise. A guide followed in order regardless of what the participant says is a survey administered out loud.

### Specifics — 10 min

Purpose: the direct questions the team needs answered, and anything that reveals what you are working on. Deliberately last so it cannot frame the earlier conversation.

This is where a concept or design can be shown, if it must be. Frame it as a task, not as a reveal: "Here's something we're exploring — what would you do on this screen?" then watch rather than ask for an opinion.

### Close — 5 min

- "Is there anything I should have asked about and didn't?" — consistently one of the highest-yield questions in the guide.
- "Anything you want to add?"
- Answer any questions they deferred earlier. You promised.
- "Is there anyone else who does this work differently that I should talk to?"
- Confirm incentive and timing.

## Why the order

Broad to narrow, and interest revealed last.

The moment a participant knows what you are working on, they start being helpful about it. Everything after that point is filtered through their model of what you want. Push that moment as late as possible, and treat answers after it as a different class of evidence.

The context section running before the core also means that by the time you ask the questions you care about, the participant has already described their real workflow. You can anchor questions to their own words instead of to your framing, which is the cheapest way to avoid leading them.

## Question bank

Adapt rather than lift. The value is in the shape.

### Opening a topic

- "Walk me through the last time you…"
- "When did you first realize you needed…"
- "How did you end up with the setup you have now?"
- "What were you doing before this tool existed?"

### Probing down

- "What happened right before that?"
- "And then what?"
- "What made you do it that way?"
- "What happens if that goes wrong?"
- "How do you handle it when that fails?"
- "Say more about that."
- *[repeat their last three words as a question]*
- *[silence, three seconds]*

### Finding the workaround

- "Is there anything you do outside the tool to make this work?"
- "Do you keep track of that anywhere else?"
- "Has anyone on your team built something for this?"
- "What do you do when it doesn't do what you need?"

A spreadsheet, a saved search, a naming convention, a private doc, a recurring reminder — each is a user-built product that measures the size of an unsolved problem.

### Sizing, anchored

- "How many times did that happen last month?"
- "Out of the last ten, how many went that way?"
- "Is this the busy season or a normal one?"

### Closing

- "What should I have asked you about?"
- "Who else does this differently?"

## Questions to strike

| Question | Why it fails | Replace with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Would you use this? | Politeness bias; no commitment behind the answer | What have you tried already? |
| Would you pay for it? | Stated willingness to pay is near-useless | What are you currently paying for this? |
| What features would you want? | Asks the participant to design | What went wrong last time? |
| Do you find X frustrating? | Contains its own answer | Walk me through the last time you did X. |
| How often do you usually…? | Invites summary, returns a guess | How many times last month? |
| Why did you do that? | Requests justification, returns a rationalization | What made you do that? |
| Don't you think it would be better if…? | Leading, and the participant will agree | *[strike entirely]* |
| Does that make sense? | Puts the participant in a position to say yes | *[strike; watch instead]* |

## Adapting the guide between sessions

The guide is expected to change. After each session, before the next:

- Cut questions that produced nothing twice in a row.
- Add the question you wished you had asked.
- Move anything that consistently comes up unprompted earlier, and note that it was unprompted — that is a finding in itself.

Keep a dated version of each guide. When synthesis finds that early and late participants answered differently, the guide history explains whether the participants differed or the questions did.
