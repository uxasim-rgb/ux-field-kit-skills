# Ethics and consent

What to say, what to record, what to keep, and where the line is.

This is a practice floor, not legal advice. Jurisdiction, sector and participant population all raise the bar above it. Where the study touches minors, health data, financial data, immigration status, or people in a dependent relationship with your organization, get a formal review.

## Informed consent, in plain language

Consent is informed when the participant could repeat back what they agreed to. A signature on a paragraph of legal text is documentation, not consent.

Cover six things before recording starts:

1. **What the session is for**, in one sentence, without selling.
2. **What is recorded** — audio, video, screen, and whether their face is captured.
3. **Who sees it** — the research team, the wider company, external clients. Be specific; "our team" is not.
4. **How long it is kept** and what happens at the end of that period.
5. **How to withdraw**, including after the session, and what withdrawal does to already-recorded material.
6. **That they can skip any question or stop at any time**, with no effect on payment.

Then start the recording and re-confirm on tape: "I've explained what we're recording and how it will be used — are you happy for me to record?" The on-record confirmation is what makes the consent auditable.

## Make withdrawal real

A withdrawal route that requires emailing a legal inbox and quoting a participant ID is a withdrawal route nobody uses. Give a named contact and a simple address, say how long they have, and honor it without asking why.

Withdrawal after synthesis is genuinely hard — the observation may already be woven into a theme. Decide the policy in advance and state it: the usual workable line is that recordings and quotes are deleted, and aggregate findings that no longer identify the participant remain.

## Record the minimum the question needs

Screen sharing is where most research data problems start. A participant sharing their real screen may expose their inbox, their files, their employer's customer data or a colleague's message. You then own that data.

- Ask them to close unrelated windows and mute notifications before sharing.
- Use a test account or synthetic data where the task allows it.
- If something sensitive appears, say so out loud, ask them to stop sharing, and note the timestamp so it can be cut.
- Do not record video of the participant unless facial reaction is genuinely part of the analysis. It rarely is, and it is the most identifying thing you could capture.

## Storage and retention

- Store recordings in a system with access control, not in a shared drive folder or a chat thread. A recording pasted into a team channel has left your retention policy.
- Separate identity from data. Keep the participant's name and contact in one place, keyed to a participant ID, and use only the ID in notes, transcripts and the repository.
- Set a retention period and actually delete. Six to twelve months covers most product work. A permanent archive of customer recordings is a liability that grows.
- Strip identifying detail from quotes before they leave the research team. Job title plus employer plus a distinctive phrase identifies someone in a small market.

## Incentives

Pay for time, not for outcome. The incentive is fixed before the session, disclosed in the invitation, and paid whether the participant completed the tasks, hated the product, or withdrew halfway.

Conditioning payment on completion selects for participants who will push through a broken flow rather than telling you it is broken, which is precisely the finding you were paying for.

Set the amount against the participant's time at their own rate, not yours. Under-paying specialist participants selects for the ones with least to do.

Disclose any relationship that could bias the answer — if the incentive comes from the team whose product is being tested, say so.

## Never use research as a sales channel

No pitching, no upselling, no "while I have you". It converts a participant into a lead, ends the relationship, and poisons the panel for every future study. If sales wants the contact, they ask for it separately, through a route the participant opted into.

The same applies in reverse: do not let a sales or success conversation be relabeled as research. The participant behaves differently when they think a commercial relationship is at stake.

## Power and dependency

Participants who depend on your organization cannot give free consent in the ordinary way: employees being studied by their own company, users on a plan whose renewal is up, participants recruited through a service they rely on.

Mitigations that work: recruit through a neutral third party, make non-participation genuinely invisible to anyone with authority over them, and say explicitly that their answers will not be attributed to them.

Mitigations that do not work: promising confidentiality in a session their manager arranged.

## When to stop a session

Stop, and say why, when:

- The participant becomes distressed. Offer to end, offer to skip, and do not push through for the data.
- Something illegal, dangerous or seriously sensitive is disclosed. Stop recording, note what happened, and escalate rather than deciding alone.
- The participant clearly misunderstood what they agreed to.
- You realize they are not the person who was screened.

Pay them regardless.

## The consent script

A working script, to adapt rather than read verbatim:

> Thanks for making the time. Before we start, a few things so you know what you're agreeing to.
>
> This is a research session, not a sales call, and nothing you say will affect your account. I'm trying to understand how you *actually* do this, so there are no wrong answers — if something is confusing, that's useful information about the product, not about you.
>
> I'd like to record the audio and your screen so I don't have to take notes the whole time. The recording goes to the product team here and nobody outside it, and we delete it after six months. I'll use short quotes in a summary, without your name.
>
> You can skip anything, and you can stop at any point — you'll still get the incentive either way. If you change your mind later, email me and I'll delete your recording.
>
> Any questions before I start recording?
>
> *[start recording]*
>
> Just for the record — I've explained how this will be recorded and used. Are you happy for me to record?
